Session 1 · Unit I

Digital platforms, bottlenecks and forced freeriding

1 Overview

Why is the news business failing, and is platform freeriding to blame?

The news industry has lost most of its revenue and newsroom jobs since about 2005. Annual newspaper revenue fell from $50 billion to $20 billion, more than 2,500 papers closed, and newsroom employment fell 60%. This session asks why. Its answer is that digital platforms capture the audience and advertising that news content attracts without paying for that content. Publishers cannot refuse to deal with them, which the course calls "forced freeriding." The session previews the legal tools the rest of the course examines: copyright, state unfair competition law, Section 230 and antitrust.

2 Readings

Stigler Center, Committee for the Study of Digital Platforms, Media Subcommittee Report (July 2019) (excerpt)

The report argues that Google and Facebook, now the largest media companies in history, have changed how news is produced and consumed. It names four concerns: the collapse of the ad-supported business model behind accountability journalism; the shift of distribution to opaque, concentrated algorithms; the lack of data on news consumption; and weak incentives for platforms to favor quality over false information. It rejects a "golden age" story and attempts to steer ad dollars back to publishers. Instead it recommends citizen-directed public funding, more transparency and procompetitive reforms.

Comments of News Corp to the FTC, Hearings on Competition and Consumer Protection in the 21st Century (Aug. 20, 2018)

Writing ahead of hearings echoing the 1995 "Pitofsky Hearings," News Corp asks the FTC to update its antitrust thinking for platforms. It argues that enforcers should look beyond price and output to quality, innovation and diversity of news. It asks the FTC to adopt presumptions suited to platform markets, to treat data as a barrier to entry, and to consider algorithm transparency. It stresses that harm to news is uniquely serious because the press supports democratic self-government.

David Pitofsky, Written Testimony to the House Judiciary Antitrust Subcommittee, "Online Platforms and Market Power, Part 1: The Free and Diverse Press" (2019)

Testifying as News Corp's general counsel, Pitofsky says the market for news is "broken." Platforms use publishers' content to target their audiences, then sell that attention to the same advertisers. He says only about 30 cents of each ad-tech dollar reaches publishers, compared with 85 cents before the internet. He also argues that Google demotes paywalled content, undercutting subscription models. Copyright alone cannot fix the problem, which is rooted in platform dominance. He supports the Journalism Competition and Preservation Act, which would let publishers bargain with platforms collectively.

A.G. Sulzberger, "A.I., Journalism and the Uncertain Future of the Public Square," remarks at the WAN-IFRA World News Media Congress (June 1, 2026)

The New York Times publisher argues that AI companies are built on a "brazen theft" of copyrighted work. They pay for talent, compute and energy but not for content, even though news sites are among their most important training sources. He says AI answers are cutting referral traffic sharply: AI chatbots send 96% less traffic than Google search, and large newspapers have lost more than 45% of their traffic. They also often misstate the news. He urges publishers to enforce their rights, license carefully, lobby for transparency and bot-identification rules, and become destinations built on original reporting.

Scanned PDF; read via OCR

Isabella Simonetti & Katherine Blunt, "News Sites Are Getting Crushed by Google's New AI Tools," Wall Street Journal (June 10, 2025)

Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode answer queries directly, sharply reducing clicks to publishers. From 2022 to 2025, organic search traffic fell by about half at HuffPost and Business Insider (55%), and nearly as much at the Washington Post. Business Insider cut 21% of its staff, and the Atlantic's CEO told staff to assume Google traffic will drop toward zero. Google says it remains committed to sending traffic to the web.

4 Key debates

Platforms or the internet itself?

Publishers blame platform freeriding and market power for the decline of news. Skeptics, including the Stigler report, point out that classified ads moved to Craigslist and job sites before platforms dominated, and that the ad-funded model was always fragile. On that view, sending ad money back to publishers would not restore journalism.

Complement or substitute?

Platforms say they send publishers valuable traffic in exchange for showing snippets. Publishers say platforms have become substitutes, and AI answers that remove the need to click make the case stronger. Much of the fight turns on how to measure the value of a referral.

Bargaining, subsidy or antitrust?

Proposals range from collective bargaining codes (Australia's News Media Bargaining Code, the U.S. Journalism Competition and Preservation Act) to public funding and licensing deals. Critics warn that bargaining codes mostly benefit large publishers, and that platforms can respond by dropping news, as Meta did in Canada in 2023.

Is journalism a public good?

If accountability journalism produces benefits readers will not pay for, markets will always underproduce it. That argument supports subsidies. Publishers prefer stronger property rights that let them charge for their work.

Session 2 · Unit II

Copyright basics and fair use

1 Overview

How much can copyright protect news when the facts are free for anyone to take?

Copyright is the publisher's first line of defense, but news gets only "thin" protection: the facts are free to all, and only the way they are written is protected. This session covers what copyright protects, what counts as infringement, and how fair use lets others copy for new purposes. It closes with the search-engine and news-monitoring cases that set up the AI fights later in the course.

2 Readings

Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone Service, 499 U.S. 340 (1991)

Feist copied listings from Rural's white pages for its own regional directory. The Supreme Court held that originality, meaning independent creation plus a "modicum of creativity," is a constitutional requirement. Facts are not original and cannot be copyrighted. A compilation is protected only in its original selection and arrangement, and alphabetical listings have none. The Court rejected the "sweat of the brow" theory, which had protected compilations because of the effort that went into them. Facts are "free for the taking."

Scanned PDF; read via OCR

Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539 (1985)

The Nation scooped Time's licensed excerpt of Gerald Ford's unpublished memoir by quoting about 300 words, including the key passages on the Nixon pardon. Time canceled its deal. The Court held this was not fair use. The work was unpublished, the quotes were the "heart" of the book, the use was a commercial scoop, and it directly destroyed a licensing market. Factor four is called the "single most important element." The Court also held there is no general public-interest exception for newsworthy works, and that bad faith weighs against fair use.

Authors Guild v. Google, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015) (Google Books)

Google scanned millions of library books to create a searchable index that shows only short "snippets." Judge Leval held this was fair use. Search and snippet display are highly transformative because they give information about books rather than substituting for them. Copying whole books was necessary for the purpose. Google's security measures and snippet limits meant the use did not provide a meaningful market substitute. The opinion marks the outer edge of transformative copying and is central to AI training defenses.

Comments of News Corp to the Copyright Office, Publishers' Protections Study (Nov. 26, 2021)

News Corp argues that U.S. law has not kept up with digital freeriding and that news deserts are the result. It lists gaps in protection: thin copyright for facts-based news; common-law misappropriation remedies limited by preemption; no way to register dynamic web content in bulk; weak anti-circumvention remedies under § 1201; and unlicensed ingestion of news for machine learning. It also points to the "unlevel playing field" with dominant platforms, and urges Congress to consider copyright, antitrust and § 230 reform together.

4 Key debates

How thin is copyright in news?

Feist leaves facts free, but news writing involves selection, framing and expression. The hard question is how close a summary can come before it takes expression. Some argue for a separate publishers' right, like Article 15 of the EU's 2019 copyright directive. Others say the free flow of facts is the point of the system.

Has transformative use gone too far?

Google Books stretched transformative use to cover copying entire works for an informational purpose. Critics say the doctrine swallowed factor four. Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023) pulled back by comparing the purposes of the two uses and weighing commerciality.

The circularity of lost licensing fees

Any use could be licensed, so counting a lost fee as market harm would defeat every fair use claim. Courts count a lost fee only where the licensing market is traditional, reasonable or likely to develop. That standard becomes decisive once AI licensing deals exist.

Search engine or substitute?

Search engines point users to the original and have won fair use cases. Clipping services and aggregators deliver the content itself and have lost. Every new news product gets argued onto one side of this line.

Session 3 · Unit II

State law claims, "hot news" and copyright preemption

1 Overview

Can state law stop rivals from taking news that copyright leaves free?

Copyright protects only how news is expressed, so publishers have turned to state law to stop rivals from taking the facts they paid to gather. This session follows "hot news" misappropriation from its 1918 origin to its near-elimination by federal preemption. It asks whether anything is left of the doctrine that could reach aggregators or AI systems that repackage news.

2 Readings

International News Service v. Associated Press, 248 U.S. 215 (1918)

INS, barred from Allied war reporting, copied AP stories from bulletin boards and early East Coast editions and wired them to its West Coast subscribers. The Supreme Court held that news is not copyrightable as such. But as between competitors, AP had a "quasi-property" interest in fresh news, and INS's taking was unfair competition: it reaped where it had not sown. Holmes would have limited relief to requiring attribution. Brandeis dissented, arguing that courts should leave the creation of new property rights in news to the legislature.

NBA v. Motorola, 105 F.3d 841 (2d Cir. 1997)

Motorola's SportsTrax pager sent real-time NBA scores and statistics, compiled by STATS reporters watching broadcasts. The Second Circuit held that game broadcasts are copyrightable but the underlying facts are not, and that most misappropriation claims are preempted. Only a narrow INS-type hot news claim survives, defined by the five-part test. NBA lost because Motorola collected the facts itself and did not free-ride on NBA's own real-time data product.

Barclays Capital v. Theflyonthewall.com, 650 F.3d 876 (2d Cir. 2011)

Investment banks sued an online service that published their stock recommendations before markets opened. The district court enjoined it on a hot news theory, but the Second Circuit reversed on preemption grounds. The banks were making the news, not gathering it, and Fly was reporting newsworthy facts under its own name rather than passing off the banks' work. The majority treated NBA's five-part test as dictum and doubted any hot news claim survives. Judge Raggi's concurrence would have applied the test and said any broader protection is for Congress.

4 Key debates

Was INS rightly decided?

The majority protected the investment in gathering news, saying no one may reap where they have not sown. Brandeis warned that courts should not create new property rights in information, and Holmes would have required only attribution. Modern scholars still divide on whether misappropriation protects incentives or creates monopolies over facts.

How much does § 301 preempt?

The Barclays majority left almost nothing of hot news. Judge Raggi would have kept the NBA test. Publishers argue courts read preemption too broadly and leave no remedy for systematic aggregation of the same content.

Who is the competitor?

Hot news assumes one rival copying one plaintiff's news. Aggregators and AI systems take from every publisher at once and compete with all of them. That makes the direct-competition element hard to apply to the conduct publishers most object to.

Courts or Congress?

Brandeis and Judge Raggi both say new rights in news belong to the legislature. The open question is whether Congress should create a federal right, as Europe did with its press publishers' right.

Session 4 · Unit II

Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act

1 Overview

Should platforms answer for what users post and what their algorithms recommend?

Section 230 shields online services from liability for content that third parties post. It lets platforms build businesses on other people's content without the legal exposure publishers face. This session traces how courts turned a narrow 1996 fix for bulletin-board moderation into broad immunity. It then asks whether that immunity covers algorithmic recommendations, and whether it could ever cover output written by generative AI.

2 Readings

Zeran v. America Online, 129 F.3d 327 (4th Cir. 1997)

An anonymous AOL user posted fake ads attributing offensive Oklahoma City bombing merchandise to Zeran. Zeran was flooded with threats, and AOL was slow to remove the posts. The Fourth Circuit held § 230 bars claims that treat a service as publisher. It read that to include "traditional editorial functions" such as deciding whether to publish, withdraw or alter content. It also rejected Zeran's argument that AOL could still be liable as a distributor once on notice. That broad reading became the national rule, and the session reviews critiques from Judge Easterbrook and Justice Thomas.

Barnes v. Yahoo!, 570 F.3d 1096 (9th Cir. 2009)

Barnes's ex-boyfriend posted fake profiles of her, and a Yahoo! employee promised to take them down but did not. The Ninth Circuit held § 230 bars her negligent-undertaking claim because removing content is publishing activity. But her promissory estoppel claim survived because it rests on Yahoo!'s own promise, not on its status as publisher. The court stresses that § 230 is not a general "immunity" but a bar on certain theories of liability.

Force v. Facebook, 934 F.3d 53 (2d Cir. 2019)

Victims of Hamas attacks alleged that Facebook's algorithms recommended Hamas content and connected users with terrorists. The majority held that arranging and recommending content is a publisher's function, and that Facebook did not "develop" the content by using neutral tools. Chief Judge Katzmann dissented in part. He argued that friend and content suggestions are Facebook's own messages, and that the legislative history aimed to protect moderation, not engagement-driven matchmaking.

Anderson v. TikTok, 116 F.4th 180 (3d Cir. 2024)

A 10-year-old died attempting the "Blackout Challenge" after TikTok's For You Page algorithm served her the videos. Relying on Moody v. NetChoice (2024), which treats platform curation as the platform's own First Amendment expression, the Third Circuit held that TikTok's algorithmic recommendations are its own first-party speech. Section 230 therefore does not bar the claims. Judge Matey's concurrence would go further, limiting § 230 to distributor-style hosting and criticizing "casual indifference" to the harm.

4 Key debates

Does "publisher" include distributor?

Reform options include repeal, targeted carve-outs like FOSTA (2018), and conditioning immunity on reasonable moderation. For publishers, § 230 gives platforms an advantage: publishers are liable for what they print, platforms are not.

Antitrust law and its application to platforms

1 Overview

Can antitrust law, built around consumer prices, reach the power of free platforms?

If copyright cannot stop freeriding, antitrust might, by attacking the platform power that lets aggregators set terms for publishers. This session covers the Sherman Act framework for single-firm conduct. It traces how the Chicago School narrowed that framework from the 1970s on. It then asks whether the doctrine can handle platforms marked by network effects, multi-sided markets and below-cost growth strategies.

2 Readings

Lina M. Khan, "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox," 126 Yale L.J. 710 (2017)

Khan argues that the consumer welfare standard, focused on short-term prices, cannot capture the harms of platforms like Amazon. Amazon gave up profits for years to gain scale. It used that scale and its control of the marketplace to favor its own products and use rivals' data, building power that low prices hide. Khan proposes reviving predatory pricing and vertical integration scrutiny, or regulating dominant platforms like public utilities. The course uses Amazon as a case study in platform phases, from matchmaking to monetization.

Erik Hovenkamp, "The Antitrust Duty to Deal in the Age of Big Tech," 131 Yale L.J. 1483 (2022)

Hovenkamp argues that courts wrongly judge all unilateral refusals to deal under one near-impossible standard. He distinguishes two kinds. "Secondary" refusals condition dealing on the counterparty's relationship with a rival, as in Lorain Journal. They work like tying or exclusive dealing and should get the same scrutiny. "Primary" refusals simply decline to share an input with a rival. Forcing those can chill investment and deserves a stricter standard. Most meritorious platform cases are secondary refusals, so separating the two would allow meaningful scrutiny of dominant platforms.

Brooke Group requires a likelihood of recoupment through later price increases. Platforms may recoup on a different side of the market or through data, which the test does not capture.

EU vs. U.S. competition law: the Amazon e-book case study

1 Overview

How differently do the EU and the U.S. treat a dominant platform's contract terms?

Amazon's e-book business is a test case for how the U.S. and the EU treat the same platform conduct. In the U.S., publishers who tried to counter Amazon's pricing power were themselves found to be cartel members. In Europe, regulators went after Amazon's contract terms instead. This session uses "most favored nation" (MFN) or parity clauses to compare the two systems and to show how platforms use contract terms in their standard-setting phase.

2 Readings

European Commission, Case AT.40153, E-book MFNs and related matters (Amazon), Decision of May 4, 2017

The Commission found preliminarily that Amazon was dominant in English- and German-language e-book retail. It found that Amazon's parity clauses may have abused that position. The clauses required publishers to offer Amazon terms matching or beating those given to rivals on business models, catalog selection, retail price, promotions and wholesale price, and to notify Amazon of better terms elsewhere. That discouraged rivals from offering new models and made entry harder. Amazon did not admit a violation. It committed for five years not to enforce or introduce the clauses, and let publishers end contracts tied to its "discount pool" provision.

This decision is the file named "Baker & Morton Article.pdf"

Jonathan B. Baker & Fiona Scott Morton, "Antitrust Enforcement Against Platform MFNs," 127 Yale L.J. 2176 (2018)

The authors argue that platform MFNs generally harm competition. They work collusively, by making discounts costly and softening price competition, and exclusionarily, by stopping new low-commission platforms from winning share through lower prices. They can be justified only where freeriding concerns are especially strong. Europe has repeatedly challenged MFNs used by online travel agents and Amazon, while the U.S. has largely ignored them. The authors argue U.S. enforcers should follow Europe, and show how cases could fit existing Sherman Act § 1 and § 2 precedent.

Commitments decisions end conduct quickly, but they produce no finding of a violation and no precedent. That leaves later cases with little guidance.

Session 7 · Unit III

Platform capture and exclusion

1 Overview

When does paying to be everyone's default become illegal exclusion?

A dominant platform can lock in its position by paying to be the default everywhere, so rivals never reach the scale they need to compete. This session studies exclusive dealing under § 2, from U.S. v. Microsoft to the DOJ's search case against Google. It then asks what remedies can actually restore competition, including whether publishers should be able to opt out of having their content used in Google's AI features.

2 Readings

United States v. Google LLC, 747 F. Supp. 3d 1 (D.D.C. Aug. 5, 2024) (Mehta, J.) (search liability decision, excerpt)

The court held that Google is a monopolist in general search services and general search text ads. It maintained those monopolies unlawfully through exclusive default agreements with Apple, Android device makers and wireless carriers, paying more than $26 billion in 2021. The agreements foreclosed a substantial share of the market and denied rivals like Bing the scale they needed. They also reduced Google's incentive to innovate and let it raise text ad prices without losing business. The court rejected Google's procompetitive justifications. It found no liability for Google's refusal to give Microsoft Ads equal features in SA360, Google's ad-buying tool, because that was a lawful refusal to deal.

Valassis Communications v. News Corp., 2019 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 27770 (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 21, 2019) (Castel, J.)

Valassis, the only rival to News America Marketing in in-store promotions such as shelf coupons and signs, alleged monopolization through exclusive dealing. News used long-term exclusive retailer contracts (averaging 4.5 years), staggered expirations, early renewals ("Project Preempt") and exclusive deals with consumer-goods makers. Valassis argued these kept it from reaching the critical mass of stores it needed, and it left the market. The court granted News summary judgment on the predatory bidding theory but denied it on the monopolization and exclusive dealing claims. The case shows platform-style exclusion in a traditional market, and it involves the course instructor's own company.

Fair use for news and information services

1 Overview

When is repackaging news a new use, and when is it a substitute for the original?

Before generative AI, courts had already decided several cases about services that copy news to deliver it in a new, more convenient form: clipping services, broadcast monitoring tools and English-language abstracts of foreign articles. This session reads those cases as the foundation for AI disputes. It asks when repackaging news is transformative and when it is just a substitute. It also introduces the first AI training ruling, Thomson Reuters v. Ross, and News Corp's position on AI.

2 Readings

Associated Press v. Meltwater U.S. Holdings, 931 F. Supp. 2d 537 (S.D.N.Y. 2013) (Cote, J.)

Meltwater scraped online news and sent subscribers keyword-based reports with each article's headline, opening sentence and other excerpts. The court rejected Meltwater's argument that it was just a search engine, finding it was a subscription news-clipping service. The use was not transformative because it simply delivered AP's expression to paying customers. Meltwater often took the article's most important passage, its "lede." It also competed directly with AP's licensed news-monitoring market. Fair use failed on every factor that mattered.

Fox News Network v. TVEyes, 883 F.3d 169 (2d Cir. 2018)

TVEyes recorded nearly all TV broadcasts and let clients paying $500 a month search transcripts and watch, archive and email ten-minute clips. The Second Circuit held the "Watch" function was not fair use. It was somewhat transformative because it helps users find and view relevant content efficiently. But it gave clients virtually all the Fox content they wanted, which deprived Fox of licensing revenue. Factors three and four strongly favored Fox. The searchable text database was not challenged on appeal. Judge Kaplan concurred, questioning whether the transformative-use analysis was needed.

Nihon Keizai Shimbun v. Comline Business Data, 166 F.3d 65 (2d Cir. 1999)

Comline sold English-language abstracts of articles from Japan's leading business paper, the Nikkei. The court applied the "more discerning observer" test, setting aside the uncopyrightable facts. It found 20 of 22 abstracts infringing because they tracked the articles' structure, sequence and much of their expression. They were essentially translations of most of the article. Two abstracts that used only facts in new words did not infringe. Fair use failed. The court affirmed the attorney's fees award but vacated the trademark injunction and remanded the statutory damages. Nihon is now central to AI output and "abridgment" claims.

Scanned PDF; read via OCR

Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence, No. 25-2153 (3d Cir. Sept. 29, 2026)

Ross used 2,243 Westlaw headnotes, rewritten as "Bulk Memos," to train a non-generative AI legal research tool meant to replace Westlaw. The Third Circuit affirmed Judge Bibas's 2025 partial summary judgment for Thomson Reuters. The headnotes are original under Feist, because editors chose which points of law to include and how to word them. Ross's use was not fair. Factor one weighed against Ross because the use was commercial and served the same purpose as Westlaw. Google Books was distinguished because Ross's tool replaces Westlaw rather than leading users to it. The intermediate copying cases (Sega, Connectix, Google v. Oracle) were distinguished because copying was not necessary: Ross could have used the public opinions themselves, and "ease is not a justification for copying." Factor four weighed against Ross because of harm to Westlaw and to a "rapidly developing" market for licensing AI training data. The court found Ross's public-access and national-security arguments unsupported.

Comments of News Corp to the Copyright Office, Artificial Intelligence and Copyright (Oct. 30, 2023)

News Corp argues that news is especially valuable LLM training data because it is high-quality, fact-checked and current, and that some developers license it (AP–OpenAI, BloombergGPT) while others simply scrape it. Copying content to build products that replace the original source is what copyright exists to prevent. News Corp opposes text-and-data-mining exceptions and mandatory licensing, and warns that dominant platforms can force publishers into "consent." It also argues that courts read § 301 preemption too broadly for aggregators and AI, which take from all publishers at once.

4 Key debates

Is convenience transformative?

TVEyes called efficient access "somewhat" transformative but still found no fair use. Critics say making the same content easier to get is just redistribution. The answer matters for any service, AI included, that repackages existing work.

Where does summary end and abridgment begin?

Nihon found abstracts that tracked the original infringing. The CIR claims in the NYT case were dismissed because bullet-point summaries restated only facts. AI answers fall somewhere between the two.

How far does Ross reach?

Ross involved a non-generative tool built to compete directly with Westlaw. Bartz and Kadrey distinguished it for generative models whose outputs do not reproduce the training works. Publishers argue news AI is closer to Ross, because answer engines compete with the sources they copy.

Is there a market for training data?

The Third Circuit in Ross found a "rapidly developing" market for licensing training data. Skeptics call that circular, since the market exists only if courts require licenses. The growing number of publisher deals with AI companies strengthens the publishers' argument.

Opt-in or opt-out?

The EU allows text-and-data mining unless rights holders opt out, and Japan's exception is broader still. U.S. fair use has no such rule. News Corp argues for an opt-in system based on licensing.

Session 10 · Unit IV

Challenges to the unauthorized use of content for LLM training

1 Overview

Is copying books and news to train AI models fair use?

Large language models are trained on huge volumes of copyrighted text, much of it copied without permission and some from pirate sites. This session examines the first major rulings on whether that training is infringement. It focuses on fair use and on the side claims authors and publishers have brought: DMCA, trademark, contributory infringement and misappropriation.

2 Readings

Bartz v. Anthropic, order on fair use (N.D. Cal. June 23, 2025) (Alsup, J.)

Judge Alsup held that training Claude on books was fair use, calling it "exceedingly transformative," like a reader learning to write. Scanning purchased print books into digital form was also fair use, because each print copy was destroyed and the digital copy was not shared. But downloading millions of pirated books to build a permanent "central library" was not fair use, and that claim went to trial. Anthropic later agreed to a $1.5 billion class settlement.

Kadrey v. Meta Platforms, summary judgment opinion (N.D. Cal. June 25, 2025) (Chhabria, J.)

Judge Chhabria granted Meta summary judgment on fair use for training Llama on 13 authors' books, but only because the plaintiffs offered no evidence of market harm. He reasoned that training is highly transformative, so plaintiffs must win decisively on factor four. He suggested "market dilution," meaning AI outputs flooding the market and competing with human works, could win in many cases, and noted news may be especially vulnerable. He rejected a licensing-market theory as circular and treated the downloads from pirate sites as relevant but not decisive. The court separately granted summary judgment for Meta on the DMCA claim.

New York Times v. Microsoft & OpenAI, 777 F. Supp. 3d 283 (S.D.N.Y. Apr. 4, 2025) (Stein, J.)

This ruling covers the consolidated NYT, Daily News and Center for Investigative Reporting suits. The court let the direct infringement claims proceed, including conduct more than three years old, under the discovery rule. It also kept the contributory infringement claims, finding plausible allegations that defendants knew users were reproducing articles, and kept the trademark dilution claims over false attributions. It dismissed with prejudice the common-law misappropriation claims as preempted and CIR's "abridgment" claims, since bullet-point summaries are not substantially similar to the articles. The DMCA § 1202 claims were mostly dismissed.

4 Key debates

Is training like learning?

Judge Alsup compared training to a reader learning to write. Judge Chhabria called that analogy inapt, because no human reader can flood the market with competing works. How courts frame training largely decides factor one.

Does the source of the copies matter?

Bartz treated the pirated library as a separate use that was not fair. Kadrey treated downloading from pirate sites as relevant but not decisive. The difference affects damages exposure across the industry, as the $1.5 billion Bartz settlement showed.

Is market dilution cognizable?

Judge Chhabria suggested AI outputs that compete with human works could weigh against fair use, even if no output copies any work. Critics say that protects style and ideas, which copyright does not cover. Supporters say it captures the real harm.

Memorization and regurgitation

The NYT complaint shows ChatGPT reproducing articles nearly word for word. OpenAI says those outputs required unusual prompts. Whether such outputs are rare edge cases or proof of substitution drives the output-based claims.

Session 11 · Unit IV

Cases involving RAG and generative AI outputs

1 Overview

Do AI answer engines that retrieve and summarize news infringe?

After training, the next battleground is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). A RAG system copies current web content into an index, retrieves articles when a user asks a question, and writes an answer from them, often with few links back to the source. That makes AI "answer engines" direct substitutes for news sites. This session asks when RAG inputs and outputs infringe, how substantial similarity works for summaries of factual news, and what liability attaches to invented content falsely credited to publishers.

2 Readings

Dow Jones & Co. and NYP Holdings v. Perplexity AI, Second Amended Complaint, No. 24-cv-7984 (S.D.N.Y. filed Jan. 28, 2025)

The publishers of the Wall Street Journal and New York Post allege that Perplexity's "answer engine," which invites users to "Skip the Links," is a substitute for their sites built on massive freeriding. Perplexity does not train its own model. Instead it crawls and scrapes publishers' pages, sometimes ignoring robots.txt, copies them into its RAG index, and feeds them into LLMs to generate answers. Those answers sometimes reproduce articles verbatim or near-verbatim, or closely paraphrase and summarize them. The complaint has four counts: (1) copyright infringement from copying works into the RAG index; (2) infringement through outputs; (3) false designation of origin under the Lanham Act; and (4) trademark dilution. The trademark counts rest on "hallucinations," meaning invented text and quotes falsely attributed to the Journal and the Post. The complaint says News Corp wrote to Perplexity in July 2024 offering to discuss licensing and got only an invitation to its revenue-sharing program. It seeks damages, an injunction and destruction of any RAG index containing the publishers' content.

4 Key debates

Is RAG more like search or like Meltwater?

Perplexity says it surfaces facts and cites sources, like a search engine. Publishers say its answers replace the visit, which it advertises with "Skip the Links." Because RAG copies content when each question is asked rather than only during training, the substitution argument is stronger than in the training cases.

Can multi-source answers avoid infringement?

An answer built from many articles may not be substantially similar to any one of them, even if together they replace the need to read any. That is the copyright gap Session 1 identified, and copyright law may not close it.

Is trademark law the right tool for hallucinations?

Publishers use the Lanham Act against invented quotes attributed to them. Dastar limits false-designation claims about authorship, so dilution and confusion theories carry more weight. Walters v. OpenAI shows the limits of defamation law for the same harm.

Does ignoring robots.txt matter legally?

Robots.txt is a voluntary standard, not a contract or an access barrier. Publishers frame scraping in violation of it as bad faith or unauthorized access. Others push technical and market fixes, such as pay-per-crawl systems and licensing.
